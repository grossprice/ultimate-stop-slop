# Claims

Write as though the reader is about to check. On a product that sells accuracy, one claim a
reader can disprove costs more than every claim they can verify earns.

## Invent nothing a reader could check

No statistics, prices, dates, awards, customer counts or quotations that did not come from a
source. Where you are unsure of a figure, say what the reader should check and where to check
it.

## Derive a number, never assert it

Three factual errors in one copy pass were the same species: a number typed into the copy
instead of read off the data it described. A page said "four failing checks" while the report
beside it showed nine checks, three of them failing.

- Read the figure off the source it describes, or compute it from that source.
- When the copy and the data disagree, the data wins and the copy changes.
- Check arithmetic in comments and briefs too. A wrong figure in a brief travels into the copy.

## A figure states what it is out of

A rate over seven samples and a rate over sixty are different claims. Put the sample beside
the figure wherever it appears: "23%, 14 of 60 checks".

`null` is not zero. "Nothing measured yet" and "measured, and the answer was none" are
different sentences.

## Quote records exactly

A quotation of a stored record, a query, an answer or a price is copied character for
character. No trailing ellipsis that presents a full string as a truncation, and no tidying.

## Absence of a claim is not evidence of absence

When a source does not mention a capability, say so: "Not on their pricing page". A cross or a
dash asserts the capability is missing, which is a claim you could not support if asked.

## No fabricated social proof

- No "most popular" on a plan nobody has bought.
- No "trusted by", "loved by", "join 10,000+ teams" without the customers to back it.
- No testimonials that were never given and no borrowed logo walls.
- Test or sample data is never worded as a real customer's result. A screenshot of the real
  interface says "this is what you get". A made-up account says "this is what someone got",
  and that is fiction.
- Invented example businesses use invented names and domains, never a real company's.

## Never claim an action the product does not take

If the product hands over a draft and the customer publishes it, the product is never the
subject of "publish". Check each verb with "we" as its subject against what the code does.

## State a claim once, where the reader commits

A claim repeated in eight places stops being a differentiator and becomes a tic. It can also
narrow the product: naming one vendor in every headline tells readers who use another vendor
that the product is not for them.

- State it where the reader is choosing (next to the input, in the plan comparison) and on the
  records it describes (provenance on each stored item).
- Everywhere else, use the general term.

## Don't imply a metric nobody measures

A count attached to a list of ideas reads as a score. If the product measures X, only X gets a
number. A list of suggestions is a list, not "12 issues".

Words carry this too. "Issues" describes a defect, which invites counting the defects. "Ideas
to try" describes the same list without the deficit.

## Don't quantify a unit that is about to change

If the pricing unit (credits, tokens, seats) is being redefined, no copy converts it ("50
credits, about 10 pages") until the definition ships.

## Free versus paid, on marketing pages

Don't elaborate on "free" ("free forever", "stays free") or contrast free with paid in
marketing copy. Inside the product, telling someone on the free plan what a paid plan adds is
the honest thing, and it stays.

## Never explain a limit by your own budget

"Our budget", "costs us real money", "we cannot afford" put your business problem on the
reader. Delete the explanation, or state the limit as a plain fact of the plan.

## Never promise a sending frequency

Consent wording, email footers and settings say what you will send, never how often. "No more
than once a month" becomes false the first month with two sends, and consent text is stored
as the record of what someone agreed to. "Occasional" carries the scope without counting.

A cadence the product keeps ("measured every 30 days") is a fact, and it stays sayable.

## Degrade openly

- Every failure names its cause. "Could not reach the site: DNS lookup failed" beats "Something
  went wrong".
- A result that returns less than hoped says so and shows what did come back.
- A hard finding reads as information, never as the reader's failure. "No shop ranks for this
  search yet, including your competitors" is the truth. Don't dress it up as a problem.

## One fact, one name

The same number carries the same name on every screen. If the dashboard calls it "conversion
rate", the report does not call it "checkout share". Pick the name the reader sees first and
use it everywhere.

Use your category's word for the unit you count. If every competitor counts "seats", a buyer
comparing tools should not have to translate your "licenses".
